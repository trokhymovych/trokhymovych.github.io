## TL;DR
I applied the open AI-text detector [mbert-ai-bot-detector](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector) to ~80K Telegram comments from July–September 2026. Combined with behavioural signals and an LLM review, it helped flag 62 likely bot accounts, two thirds of them caught only by the [AI-text detector](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector). Not every bot writes AI text, so the detector works best alongside other signals.

**Коротко українською:** Я застосував відкритий детектор ШІ-тексту [mbert-ai-bot-detector](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector) до ~80 тис. коментарів у телеграм-чаті за липень–вересень 2026 року. Разом із поведінковими ознаками та перевіркою мовною моделлю він допоміг виявити 62 ймовірні бот-акаунти, дві третини з яких знайдено лише завдяки [детектору](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector). Проте не всі боти пишуть ШІ-текстом, тож детектор найкраще працює разом з іншими сигналами.


## Motivation
Can you tell whether a comment on social media was written by a real person or generated by an AI? 
Well, you definitely can have a good guess, but it is not easy.
This case study is for anyone who works with comment sections: researchers, moderators and journalists. 
It shows how the open model [**mbert-ai-bot-detector**](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector), from our CIKM 2026 paper [*Adversarial Creation and Detection of AI-Generated Social Bot Content*](/publications/adversarial-creation-and-detection-of-ai-generated-social-bot-content-2026/), can help find AI-bot accounts in a real Ukrainian-language Telegram chat.

Writing a fluent, on-topic comment used to take a person minutes. A large language model does it in a fraction of a second, in any language and in any tone. That makes it cheap to fill a comment section with accounts that look like ordinary people. 

What are the consequences? Chats can be flooded with AI-generated comments that push specific narratives, drown out dissent and make it look like a topic is more popular than it really is. A single AI comment can be enough to start a conversation, and a few hundred can make it look like a movement.

## About the detector

[mbert-ai-bot-detector](https://huggingface.co/trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector) is an open text classifier on Hugging Face. It reads one text and returns the probability that it was written by an AI-powered social bot (`LABEL_1`) rather than a person (`LABEL_0`). In this post I call that probability the **AI score**: 0 looks human, 1 looks generated.

- **Base model:** multilingual BERT (`bert-base-multilingual-cased`), covering 104 languages, fine-tuned on data in 17 languages.
- **Training idea:** the paper generates adversarial bot content with LLMs and trains the detector to catch it. On the paper's Fox-8-23 benchmark it reaches a user-level AUC of 0.989.
- **Intended use:** judge *accounts* by aggregating scores over many of their texts. The model card recommends 20 or more texts per account. A single short text is a weak signal.

**Why this model for this case:** it is open, runs on a laptop GPU (or even a CPU), handles 17 languages including Ukrainian and Russian without translation, and was built for social-media bot text rather than essays. Short, slangy chat in two mixed languages is also a fair stress test.

### How to use the detector? 
```bash
pip install transformers torch
```

```python
from transformers import pipeline

detector = pipeline("text-classification",
                    model="trokhymovych/mbert-ai-bot-detector",
                    truncation=True, max_length=512)

def ai_score(texts):
    """Probability that each text was written by an AI-powered bot."""
    return [r["score"] if r["label"] == "LABEL_1" else 1 - r["score"]
            for r in detector(texts, batch_size=32)]

# Two (lightly edited) comments from the case study (both replies to the same channel post)
comments = [
    "Ще один завод на цвинтар, а влада мобілізацію посилює, капець",
    "Так житомир то попереджали . Незабувайте нас в це втягнув зелений.",
]
for text, score in zip(comments, ai_score(comments)):
    print(f"{score:.2f}  {text}")
```

For an account, score all of its comments, keep the longer ones and look at the mean:

```python
long_comments = [c for c in account_comments if len(c) >= 40]
scores = ai_score(long_comments)
mean_score = sum(scores) / len(scores)   # >= 0.5 over several long messages: worth a closer look
```

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Why each step</b></summary>

Each step exists because of something I saw in the data:

1. **Score every message.** It is cheap, automatic and the strongest single signal I have.
2. **Judge accounts, not messages.** Short messages are unreliable. I classify accounts with at least 5 comments, compute AI features on messages of 40+ characters, and compare each score with messages of similar length. This follows the model card: aggregate over many texts.
3. **Look beyond AI text.** Not every bot uses an LLM. Some paste the same human-written slogan a hundred times. For each account I therefore also measure repetition, identical or rephrased texts shared with other accounts, bursts of activity, activity while the chat is quiet, account lifetime and co-commenting with the same partners.
4. **Flag candidates.** An account is flagged when it shows content evidence (AI text, repetition or copying) or matches a concrete rule. One rule relies on the detector alone: if the long messages of an account average an AI score of **0.5 or more**, it is always flagged. Behaviour alone, such as posting a lot, never flags anyone, because very active people are common.
5. **Review the full account.** A local LLM (gemma4:26b, run with Ollama) gets a dossier of each flagged account. The dossier contains every measurement with its percentile in the chat, the highest- and lowest-scoring messages, timing, copies and co-commenting partners side by side, topics and a comment timeline. It answers *bot*, *not bot* or *unsure*. This removes false alarms that numbers alone cannot. For example, many people in Kyiv comment at 3 a.m. during air-raid alerts, so night activity looks suspicious until you see the whole chat is awake. The reviewer never decides whether a text is AI-written; that comes only from the detector.

</details>

### Three account stories

**The burner.** Account 3155 appeared, posted 10 long comments in about six minutes and was never seen again. Their mean AI score is 0.92. The LLM reviewer labelled it *automated cadence*.

{% include figure.html path="assets/img/blog/ai-bot-detection-telegram/account-story-burner.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="Scatter of AI scores over six minutes: nine of ten messages score between 0.87 and 1.00" caption="Figure 1. The burner: ten comments in six minutes, almost all scored as generated." %}

**The switcher.** Account 5363 first wrote human-like comments. Then, at one point, the mean AI score of their long messages jumped from **0.13 to 0.72** and stayed there. Nobody told the detector about dates; the change shows up in its scores alone. The LLM reviewer labelled the account as a bot pushing one narrative.

{% include figure.html path="assets/img/blog/ai-bot-detection-telegram/account-story-switcher.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" zoomable=true alt="AI scores for Account 5363 over time: low scores at first, then mostly between 0.85 and 1.00" caption="Figure 2. The switcher: human-like at first, generated-looking afterwards." %}

**The persistent agent.** Account 5841 posted ~140 comments in ~5 weeks, about 20 per active day. Their long messages average **0.86**, and 80% score above 0.8. The LLM reviewer found near-identical content shared with other suspicious accounts and labelled it *coordinated*.

### Not every bot is an AI bot

Among the 62 LLM-confirmed bots, **73%** were identified through AI-like text and **27%** through repetition, copying or other rules only. Without the detector, **42 of the 62 LLM-confirmed bots (68%)** would not have been flagged at all. Other signals catch copy-paste and template bots. Only the text model catches a well-written, one-off generated comment.

A few more patterns that stood out:
- **AI-like text:** 27.9% of long messages from likely-bot accounts score above 0.8, against 6.5% for everyone else.
- **Account age:** 24% of LLM-confirmed bots are among the newest 10% of account IDs, against 10% of normal accounts. Telegram IDs grow over time, so this is a rough age proxy.
- **People mostly ignore them:** bots wrote 3.6% of comments but received only 1.1% of replies and 2.6% of reactions.

### The hidden part: deleted messages

The export shows the chat *after* moderation. Telegram numbers the messages of a chat sequentially, and in my export the IDs increase with time in 100% of cases. So every gap in the numbering is a message that no longer exists.

- **16.8%** of message IDs in the period are missing (~16K of ~98K). This is an upper bound: it includes deletions by authors, admins and antispam.
- **~2.4K comments** come from accounts that have since been deleted. That happened to **8% of LLM-confirmed bots**, against 3% of other accounts.
- On the "worst" day, 55% of the messages were removed.

**Why it matters:** everything in this post is a **lower bound**. Moderators and Telegram had already removed part of the bot activity before I looked.

### Interpreting the AI score

- **Single messages:** treat a score above 0.8 as "AI-like", but only for messages of 40+ characters. On 20 to 39 characters, the share of high scores is the same (17%) for bots and everyone else, so very short texts carry no signal.
- **Accounts:** a mean of 0.5 or more over several long messages is a good reason to review an account, not a verdict. Here, 12% of accounts flagged by the AI signal alone were LLM-confirmed after review. With 5+ comments per account I stayed below the model card's recommendation of 20+ texts; more text per account gives more reliable decisions.
- **Calibration:** compare scores within the same length band, and against the community's own baseline.

**Typical failure modes:**

- **Very short messages**, even from regular human members, can score high.
- **Humans who write with an AI assistant** look like bots in the scores.
- **Human-written spam and copy-paste** is not AI text; use repetition and copying signals for it.
- **Out-of-domain text,** such as quoted news or code, is not what the model was trained on.

## Limitations and responsible use

- **Lower bound, not a census.** Accounts with fewer than 5 comments are not classified, and part of the activity was deleted before export.
- **No external ground truth.** "LLM-confirmed" means confirmed by an LLM reviewer that saw the detector's verdict, not verified independently. A blinded re-run and a human-labelled sample are needed.
- **One chat, three months.** The results describe this community, not Telegram as a whole.
- **Generated is not malicious.** Do not use the AI score alone to ban or name anyone. Use it to decide where a human should look.
